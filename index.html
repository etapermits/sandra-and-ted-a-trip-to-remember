Sandra & Ted,

I wanted to put something together for you that was a little more than a booking confirmation — something you might actually use, enjoy, and perhaps look back on long after you've come home.

Ireland in September is, in my view, one of the finest things a person can do. The summer crowds have thinned, the light has turned that particular amber that Irish painters have been chasing for centuries, and the whole island seems to exhale. You've chosen the right month without even knowing it.

What makes your trip especially special is the family roots. There is something quite profound about standing on Irish soil for the first time when part of you was always there — in a name, a story passed down, a face in an old photograph. Ireland has a way of meeting you halfway on that. People will ask where you're from, and when the conversation turns to family, you'll find they take it seriously. The Irish have long memories for these things.

I should also tell you — and this is a lovely coincidence — that you arrive on the 19th of September, which means you'll be in Ireland right in the heart of the Lisdoonvarna Matchmaking Festival in County Clare. For the entire month of September, a small village of 800 people on the Wild Atlantic Way transforms into the world's largest singles festival — 165 years old, 60,000 visitors, music and céilí dancing from noon until the small hours every single day. Even if matchmaking isn't on your agenda, go for the evening, have a pint, watch people dance, and let Willie Daly — Ireland's last professional matchmaker — tell you your fortune from his ancient lucky book. You'll talk about it for years.

I've put together a complete guide for your trip — not a tourist brochure, but somewhere between a personal recommendation and a proper companion for the road. It covers the places most visitors never find, the coastlines that make even the Irish go quiet, the pubs where the music has been playing for forty years, the farmstays where breakfast comes from the farm outside your window, and the cathedrals that carry 1,500 years of history in their stones.

I'd suggest somewhere between ten days and three weeks — it really depends on your pace and how deeply you want to go. Ten days gives you a wonderful taste if time is tight. Two weeks lets you breathe and explore both north and south properly. Three weeks means you can genuinely get lost in it — and getting lost in Ireland is highly recommended. The guide covers enough for all three, so take what suits you and leave the rest for next time.

One thing I am certain of — this will be the experience of a lifetime. The kind of trip that stays with you long after the suitcases are unpacked. The stories, the landscapes, the music, the people — Ireland has a way of getting under your skin like nowhere else. You'll be dining out on this one for years, sharing it with family and friends, and wondering when you can go back.

Enjoy every single moment of it.

With warm regards,
Philip

"Ireland is not a land you visit. It is a land that visits you — long after you have gone home."
There is a quality to the light in Ireland that painters have tried to capture for centuries and never quite managed. It falls differently here — soft and silver in the west, golden over the midland bogs, diamond-sharp on the northern coast after rain. Perhaps that is why, of all the countries on earth, Ireland produces more poets per head of population than almost any other. There is simply too much beauty to remain silent about.
Ireland is an island of extraordinary contrasts. In a single day you can stand on sea cliffs three times higher than the Cliffs of Moher with nobody else in sight, eat the finest oysters in Europe in a medieval village, walk through Neolithic monuments older than the pyramids, and end the evening in a pub where the fiddle music started forty years ago and has barely paused since.
What makes Ireland different from almost every other destination in Europe is that the beauty is not managed or packaged. The wild Atlantic still pounds the same cliffs it always has. The bogs of Connaught still smell of peat smoke on a wet afternoon. A farmer will stop his tractor on a narrow road to have a conversation with a stranger, because in Ireland time is not something that is saved — it is spent, freely, on whoever happens to be in front of you.
The culture runs deeper still. Irish is the oldest written vernacular language in Europe — older than French, older than Spanish. Traditional music played on fiddle, uilleann pipes, tin whistle and bodhrán is not a heritage display but a living tradition, performed in pub corners on wet Tuesday evenings by people who learned from their grandparents.
The island is divided politically — the Republic of Ireland in the south, Northern Ireland in the north as part of the UK. But in landscape, culture, music and hospitality the border is entirely invisible. This guide covers the whole island, because the island cannot meaningfully be divided. Cross freely and often.
The Irish have a word — craic (pronounced "crack") — that does not translate into any other language. It means something between fun, conversation, atmosphere and the particular electricity of a room where good things are happening. "Great craic" is the highest compliment a night out can receive.

Traditional Irish music is not a performance. It is a conversation. Musicians gather in pub corners, play for each other and for whoever is listening. No stage, no microphone. You walk in and the music is simply there — fiddle, uilleann pipes, flute, tin whistle, bodhrán. The céilí (pronounced "kay-lee") is Ireland's communal dance — jigs and reels in groups, led by a caller. No experience needed. No partner necessary. You will be swept up.

Ireland's sacred architecture spans 5,000 years — from Neolithic passage tombs aligned with the winter solstice, to round towers built by early Christian monks, to Gothic cathedrals that took generations to complete. Holy wells, pilgrimage mountains and ancient monastic islands dot the landscape, many still actively visited by pilgrims today. Even the ruin of a medieval abbey on a misty morning carries a weight of silence that is unlike anything else in Europe.

Waterford Festival of Food, Dungarvan

Lisdoonvarna Matchmaking Festival, Co Clare
All of September · 165 years old · Sandra & Ted will be here!
You arrive right in the middle of this. The world's largest singles festival transforms a small Clare village for the entire month. These are the foods and drinks that define Ireland — some protected by EU law, others simply legendary. Part of what makes an Irish trip memorable is the food. Seek these out wherever you find them.
F1–F7 · Irish food & drink specialities
F1
Waterford Blaa
EU Protected · Same status as Champagne
A doughy, floury bread roll unique to Waterford — so protected it has EU PGI status like Champagne. Only four licensed bakeries in the world can make them. Eaten for breakfast filled with butter and rashers. Try Hickey's Bakery or Walsh's in Waterford city.
F2
Carlingford Oysters, Co Louth
Cold lough waters · Among the finest in Europe
Grown in the cold, clean tidal waters of Carlingford Lough. Among the finest oysters in Europe. Try them at PJ O'Hare's pub in Carlingford village (Stop 9) with a pint of stout. A perfect combination.
F3
Connemara Lamb, Co Galway
EU Protected · Mountain-reared on heather & wild herbs
Mountain-reared on wild Connemara bogland. The flavour — from foraging on heather, wild herbs and saltgrass — is unmistakable. EU PGI. Try at Ballynahinch Castle Hotel restaurant near Recess, Connemara.
F4
Burren Artisan Produce, Co Clare
St Tola goat's cheese · Burren Smokehouse salmon
The Burren's unique limestone landscape produces extraordinary food. St Tola goat's cheese (one of Europe's finest), Burren Smokehouse salmon, and wild garlic gathered from the limestone pavements. The Burren Slow Food Festival includes a four-course banquet sourced entirely locally.
F5
Longueville House Cider & Apple Brandy
Estate-produced · French oak aged · Extraordinary
Produced from the Longueville estate's own orchards (Farmstay FS2). The apple brandy is aged in French oak casks and is quite extraordinary for an Irish product. Available to buy directly from the estate — a wonderful gift to bring home.
Phone+353 22 47156 · longuevillebeverages@gmail.com
F6
The Perfect Pint of Guinness
119.5 seconds · Best in Dublin
A properly poured pint takes exactly 119.5 seconds and the difference is very noticeable. The best pints in Dublin are at Neary's on Chatham Street or Mulligan's on Poolbeg Street — widely regarded as the finest in the capital. Do not rush the pour.
F7
Irish Whiskey Distillery Trail
40+ distilleries today — was just 1 in 2010
Ireland's whiskey revival is one of the great food stories of the last decade. Key stops: Waterford Distillery (single farm origin — unique in the world), Dingle Distillery (Kerry), Slane Distillery (next door to Rock Farm Farmstay FS4). All offer tours and tastings.
